A. The problem and the low-fidelity model

A small electric aircraft carries a fixed payload. The study trades endurance against take-off mass over six design inputs. The low level is a vortex lattice, a closed-form section, a drag build-up, a mass closure and an energy balance; it costs well under a second per design, so the whole box can be evaluated. These entries are the physics of section 2 of the deck and the solver checks V6a to V6d.

Definition

The study sizes a small electric fixed-wing aircraft that carries a fixed payload of 0.5 kg (payload_mass_kg in data/drone/rules.yaml). It has two objectives: make the endurance long and make the take-off mass small. A longer wing and a bigger battery give more endurance, but they also add mass. So the answer is a Pareto front and not one point. The code minimizes \(f_1 = -t_{\mathrm{end}}\) and \(f_2 = \log m_{\mathrm{TO}}\) (spec section 5).

A regulation sets a hard line: the EASA open category, class C2, allows at most 4.0 kg take-off mass (mass_cap_kg, Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/945). The cap was fixed in the spec before any run. Question 4 of the spec asks for the longest endurance with \(P(m_{\mathrm{TO}} \le 4.0\ \mathrm{kg}) \ge 0.9\).

Six inputs span the design box. All six are surrogate inputs. Everything else is a fixed quantity from the ledger (entry 2). The cruise speed is not an input. Each design flies at the faster of two speeds: the speed of least power on its own drag polar, and 1.3 times its own stall speed. An optimizer would always drive a free cruise speed to the same place, so a rule removes one dimension. The flight condition is sea-level ISA air only.

Interview questions
Why is the cruise speed not one of your inputs?

I fix it by a rule: the faster of the least-power speed and 1.3 times the stall speed. An optimizer that maximizes endurance would always push a free cruise speed to that same place, so a seventh input would only add a dimension the surrogate must learn for nothing. The rule is in spec section 2, and cruise_point builds it by clamping the CL scan at \(C_{L,\max}/1.3^2\). At the nominal design the stall margin is the active bound, at 9.04 m/s.

The 4 kg cap is the whole point of question 4. Does it bind?

Not on the low-fidelity model. The heaviest of the 192 low-pool designs is 3.57 kg, so the low-fidelity inverse answer is simply the longest-endurance buildable corner. I say this on the slide, because it means question 4 is only tested at the high level. There it nearly binds: on all 32 SU2 designs the answer has a median mass of 3.918 kg with \(P(m \le 4) = 0.991\) (runs/drone_inverse_hf/2026-11-09/inverse.json), and the confirmation run gave 3.936 kg, under the cap (entry 23).

Span and aspect ratio: \(S = b^2/AR\). Taper then splits the area into root and tip chords.

It is the code's choice, recorded in addendum 3 of the spec. The two differ by a few percent over the box's tapers (0.2042 m against 0.20 m at the nominal design).

Definition

Every quantity that is not a design input lives in one ledger: five YAML files under data/drone/, one per block. Each quantity has a name, a value, units, a grade and a source. The grade is one of three words. Published: a value from a cited standard, regulation or textbook. Derived: a value this study defines or computes, such as a gate limit. Assumed: no source gives the value, so the source string is a rationale, and the quantity must carry a band [lo, hi] that contains the value.

The bands are not surrogate inputs. The surrogate learns only the six design inputs (entry 1). The effect of each band is measured once, by a one-at-a-time sweep of the low-fidelity model at one design (spec section 10, item 5), and it is shown as a ranked table (D39, DB8). Nothing in the ledger is typed from a run result: the ledger is fixed before the runs. The validation record (ledger_hashes in v6.json) and the grid and pool records (ledger_sha256 in their record.json) store the sha256 of each ledger file.

One caution to know before the interview. Many "published" entries say in their own source string that the document was cited and not retrieved in the sandbox (for example ISA air, the C2 cap, the FAR Part 23 load factor). stall_margin even says "cited from memory of the regulation". So "published" here means "a standard value with a named primary source", not "a document we hold and have read".

Interview questions
How do you stop someone from slipping an unsourced number into the model?

Every constant enters the model through drone/ledger.py, and the loader raises on a quantity with no source, no grade, or an assumed value with no band. The model functions take the loaded ledger as an argument, so there is no second place to hide a constant. The validation, grid and pool records store the sha256 of every ledger file, so I can show which ledger produced which number. The generated assumptions document is byte-checked against the YAML by a test.

Your lift-effectiveness band does not contain what the tunnel says. Why not fix it?

Because moving it would be tuning the low level to the validation data. V6f is a report row by the spec, and its job is to size the error the two-level model has to learn. It is still an open ruling, with both states on the record; the rulings taken since, decisions 9 and 10, were only on budget-exhausted SU2 runs.

Definition

A vortex-lattice method (VLM) replaces a thin wing by a grid of panels. Each panel carries one horseshoe vortex: a bound leg at the quarter chord of the panel and two trailing legs that run to infinity downstream. Each horseshoe has an unknown strength \(\Gamma_j\). The velocity that every horseshoe induces at a point comes from the Biot-Savart law for a straight vortex segment. The flow must not cross the surface, so at the three-quarter-chord control point of each panel the total velocity has no component along the panel normal. This gives one linear equation per panel. The solver in the repository solves the dense system with numpy.linalg.solve. The left wing is the mirror image of the right one, so only the right half is solved.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{seg}} &= \frac{\Gamma}{4\pi}\,\frac{\mathbf{r}_1 \times \mathbf{r}_2}{\lvert \mathbf{r}_1 \times \mathbf{r}_2 \rvert^2}\; \mathbf{r}_0 \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{r}_1}{\lvert \mathbf{r}_1 \rvert} - \frac{\mathbf{r}_2}{\lvert \mathbf{r}_2 \rvert} \right) \\ \left( \mathbf{V}_\infty + \sum_j \mathbf{A}_{ij}\,\Gamma_j \right) \cdot \mathbf{n}_i &= 0 \qquad \text{at every control point } i \\ \mathbf{F}_j &= \Gamma_j\, (\mathbf{V}_\infty + \mathbf{v}_j) \times \mathbf{l}_j \qquad \text{(Kutta-Joukowski, near field)} \end{aligned} \]

  • _forces solves for \(\Gamma\), gets \(C_L\) and \(C_m\) from Kutta-Joukowski on each bound leg, and gets \(C_{D_i}\) in the Trefftz plane from the strip circulations fitted to Glauert's sine series. Interview questions
How do you know the vortex lattice is right?

Three kinds of check, in v6.json. An elliptic wing must give \(e\) close to 1 and the Helmbold and lifting-line slopes (V6a: -0.26 and -1.95 percent). A published benchmark, Warren 12, gives the slope to +1.01 percent (V6b). Coarse, medium and fine lattices must agree (V6c: 0.17 percent in the slope). I did not tune anything to make a row pass.

Why is the induced drag taken from the Trefftz plane and not from the forces on the panels?

The near-field force on the bound legs is a small difference of large terms. The Trefftz-plane drag depends only on the strip circulations. The point-vortex version of the far-field sum was tried first and under-counts by about 1/N, so the code fits a Glauert sine series to the strip circulations, whose drag is exact.

Self-check
Where does camber enter the lattice?

Only through the panel normals: \(\mathbf{n} \propto (-dz/dx, 0, 1)\). The panels stay in the plane z = 0.

Why is a flat wing at zero angle of attack exactly zero lift in this solver?

The right-hand side of the linear system is zero, so \(\Gamma = 0\) and \(C_L = C_{D_i} = 0\) exactly.

Definition

The vortex lattice (entry 3) gives the wing's lift distribution, but it has no viscosity. The section data add what the lattice cannot know: the section's maximum lift \(c_{l,\max}\), its zero-lift angle \(\alpha_{L0}\) and its profile drag \(c_d\). This block is the only one that differs between the two fidelity levels.

At the low level the section comes from closed forms. The NACA four-digit shape is the published formula (Abbott and von Doenhoff). The zero-lift angle is thin-airfoil theory applied to the NACA camber line. The maximum lift assumes the section keeps a fraction \(k_{\mathrm{eff}}\) of the thin-airfoil slope \(2\pi\) up to a fixed stall angle. The profile drag is flat-plate skin friction on both surfaces times a thickness form factor. It is one constant: it does not change with \(c_l\). The section docstring names this as a bias, not an oversight.

The Reynolds number enters at the low level through the skin friction only: \(c_d \propto Re^{-1/2}\) below 5e5 (Blasius, laminar) and \(Re^{-1/5}\) above (Prandtl-Schlichting, turbulent). \(c_{l,\max}\) and \(\alpha_{L0}\) do not depend on Re at this level. The section is evaluated at the cruise Reynolds number on the mean aerodynamic chord, one Re per design; this is a stated bias, because the chord varies along a tapered span.

At the high level, from_polar takes an SU2 polar instead (block B): \(c_{l,\max}\) is the largest computed \(c_l\), the slope and \(\alpha_{L0}\) come from a straight-line fit to the points below 0.85 \(c_{l,\max}\), and \(c_d(c_l)\) is interpolated.

Definition

The drag polar \(C_D(C_L)\) of the whole aircraft is the sum of three parts, all on the wing area \(S\). Induced drag comes from the lattice through the span efficiency \(e\) (entry 3). Wing profile drag comes from the section: the section \(c_d\) at each strip's own \(c_l\), integrated over the span with the lattice's spanwise loading. Parasite drag of the fuselage and the two tail surfaces comes from flat-plate skin friction times a Raymer form factor times the wetted area. The tails are sized by volume coefficients and the fuselage is an ellipsoid that holds the battery and the payload, so every wetted area follows from the six inputs and the closed mass.

The spec names the biases that travel with every figure (section 13): no trim drag (it flatters every design about equally to first order); fully turbulent skin friction on the non-wing parts above Re 5e5; the fixed propulsive efficiency; the closed-form structure; and the section polar at one Reynolds number per design while the chord varies along the span. Section 3 adds: no compressibility (Mach below 0.1). The low-level section adds one more: its profile drag does not change with \(c_l\) (entry 4).

What drag is missing from your build-up, and which way does it push the answer?

Self-check
Why does the wing profile drag equal the section \(c_d\) exactly at the low level?

The section \(c_d\) is the same at every strip, and \(2\sum c_i \Delta y_i = S\), so the integral divided by \(S\) gives back the constant.

It jumps from the laminar to the turbulent law: at Re 5e5 the laminar value is 0.00188 and the turbulent value is 0.00536. The switch is a step, not a blend, so a part whose Re crosses it has a step in its drag.

Interview questions
Why iterate, and why does it converge?

The mass is the unknown on both sides, so there is no direct formula. The map from one mass to the next is nearly flat, because the battery term has slope \(f_b\) (below 1) and the other terms grow more slowly than \(m\). I do not prove this in the code. It is a stated bias that travels with every figure: it sizes two caps for the root bending moment of an elliptic lift distribution and ignores torsion, buckling and the weight of the skin on the bending.

Definition

At a chord Reynolds number of \(10^5\) to \(3 \times 10^5\) the boundary layer on a thin airfoil starts laminar. Behind the suction peak the pressure rises. The laminar layer can separate there, turn turbulent in the separated shear layer and reattach. This is a laminar separation bubble. It changes the lift and the drag, and it moves as the angle of attack changes. Plain Spalart-Allmaras (SA_OPTIONS= NONE, the SU2 default) is turbulent from the leading edge, so it has no bubble. So the case template switches on the Bas-Cakmakcioglu option (SA-BCM). BCM multiplies the production term of the Spalart-Allmaras equation by an intermittency between 0 and 1. The intermittency is an algebraic function of local flow quantities and of the freestream turbulence intensity, so the production stays near zero in the laminar region and switches on where transition occurs. The solver is SU2 v8.2.0 "Harrier", SOLVER= INC_RANS: steady, incompressible, constant density and viscosity, on a structured C-mesh (entry 10). A moving bubble means the steady solution may not settle. That is a property of the flow and not only of the iteration budget (entry 11).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Reynolds number: } & Re = \frac{\rho V c}{\mu} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = \frac{Re\,\mu}{\rho\,c}, \qquad c = 1\ \mathrm{m} \\ \text{freestream: } & \mathbf{V}_\infty = V\,(\cos\alpha,\ \sin\alpha), \qquad \texttt{AOA} = \alpha \ \text{(the mesh is never rotated)} \\ \text{SA-BCM production: } & P = \gamma_{BC}\, c_{b1}\, \tilde S\, \tilde\nu, \qquad 0 \le \gamma_{BC} \le 1, \quad \gamma_{BC} = f(\text{local flow},\ Tu) \end{aligned} \]

Interview questions
Why SA-BCM and not plain Spalart-Allmaras or a full transition model?

I need a laminar region and a separation bubble, because that is the physics at Re 1e5 to 3e5. Plain SA is turbulent from the leading edge, so it has neither. BCM adds transition to SA with one algebraic intermittency and no extra transport equation, so the cost stays close to SA. The spec allowed SST with Langtry-Menter (template inc_sst_lm_airfoil.cfg) as a second attempt. It was not run: BCM failed the lift slope, and decision 8 accepted that known bias rather than trading it for an unmeasured one (docs/validation/2026-11-08_task10_report.md).

How does the case know the Reynolds number if you never set it?

I set it through the velocity. The chord is 1 m, density and viscosity come from the ledger, so \(V = Re\,\mu/(\rho c)\). The config writes that velocity along the angle of attack. The header of every case.cfg prints the formula and the value, so a reader can check it.

Definition

The mesh is one structured C-grid of quadrilaterals around the section. Index i runs round the C: along the lower wake cut, round the lower surface, the leading edge, the upper surface and back out along the upper wake cut. Index j runs away from the wall. The grid is cut into four blocks: lower wake, lower wall, upper wall, upper wake. The farfield is 30 chords out: a semicircle round mid-chord, joined to the lines y = ±30 and the outflow line 30 chords behind the trailing edge. Each wall column is a cubic Hermite curve that leaves the wall along its normal, with a fixed first spacing \(d_0\) and one geometric ratio. \(d_0\) comes from a flat-plate rule for \(y^+ = 0.5\). Three levels differ by a node-count factor of 1.4 per direction. Before a case is written, the mesh must pass the mesh-quality gate in worker/make_case.py: no inverted cell, minimum angle, first-cell height, growth and aspect ratio. A failing mesh is never written and never submitted.

Definition

Two rules decide when a run is done, and they measure different things. SU2's Cauchy rule stops the solver. It looks at the relative change of the lift and drag coefficients from one iteration to the next, averaged over the last 200 iterations, and stops when the average is below \(10^{-5}\) for both, after iteration 500. The repository's drift rule classifies the run afterwards. It takes the range (max minus min) of a coefficient over the last 20 percent of the iterations, divided by the final value. A run is converged only if the residual dropped at least 5 orders and every drift is below 0.2 percent. A slowly settling case can pass the Cauchy rule, because the change per iteration is tiny, and still fail the drift rule, because 20 percent of a long run covers a real change. That is why every airfoil2d run reports not_converged. Both numbers are kept on every record and neither rule was loosened (decision 6).

Definition

The pressure coefficient compares the local wall pressure with the freestream dynamic pressure. On a lifting section the upper surface has a minimum of Cp near the leading edge: the suction peak. Behind it the pressure rises toward the trailing edge, an adverse gradient. A laminar layer separates easily in an adverse gradient. Inside the separated region the pressure stays nearly constant, so the Cp curve shows a short plateau, and then a fast recovery where the flow becomes turbulent and reattaches. The skin-friction coefficient is the wall shear over the dynamic pressure. Its x component is negative where the wall shear points upstream, that is, where the flow at the wall is reversed. A stretch of negative \(C_{f,x}\) between a separation point and a reattachment point is the mark of a laminar separation bubble. As the angle rises the suction peak deepens and moves forward, and the bubble moves forward with it.

Key relations
\[ \begin{aligned} C_p &= \frac{p - p_\infty}{\tfrac12 \rho V^2}, \qquad C_{f,x} = \frac{\tau_{w,x}}{\tfrac12 \rho V^2} \\ \text{separation / reattachment: } & C_{f,x} = 0 \ \text{with a sign change}; \qquad \text{reversed flow: } C_{f,x} \lt 0 \\ c_l &\approx \oint C_p\, \hat n \cdot \hat e_L \, d(s/c) \quad \text{(pressure part; friction adds little to } c_l\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Definition

Stall is the loss of lift when the flow separates from most of the upper surface. The suction peak collapses, the upper-surface Cp becomes nearly flat, the lift falls and the pressure drag rises sharply. The low-fidelity section in drone/section.py is a closed form: a straight lift line from thin-airfoil theory, scaled by a lift-effectiveness factor, capped at a \(c_{l,\max}\), with a constant profile drag. A cap is not a stall. Past the cap the closed form holds \(c_l\) flat and its drag never rises, so it cannot show the drop in lift or the drag rise. The SU2 section can, which is one reason the high level exists.

Definition

A mesh study runs the same case on meshes of increasing resolution and reads how much the answer moves. If the answer moves less and less, monotonically, the finest result is close to the mesh-independent value, and Richardson extrapolation can estimate the remaining error. Here the same case (nominal section, Re 200,000, 6 degrees, SA-BCM) was run at three levels. The shifts are small, but they are not monotone in cell count, and they are smaller than the fine run's own drift. So the study resolves the drift band and not the mesh error. On that evidence the pool runs on the coarse mesh for cost (decision 7). A bias check then runs the first four pool designs at 2, 6 and 10 degrees on the medium mesh as well, and compares case by case.

Definition

A budget-exhausted run is one that used all its iterations (6,000) without SU2's Cauchy rule stopping it. It exits 0, so it looks finished, but the solver did not judge it settled. By default the polar aggregator drops such runs; it keeps runs that SU2 stopped on its own. The record keeps every dropped run with its drift. Whether some of them should be accepted was ruling 1 in the Task 10 report, and it is now RULED, by Éder and not by the deck. Decision 9 (2026-11-10 01:35 UTC) accepts the budget-exhausted runs of pool designs 8 and 28 only. Decision 10 (2026-11-10 02:20 UTC) accepts one more case only: the 14-degree confirmation case airfoil2d-84a1950ba6 of the 30-design answer. For every other run the default is unchanged: dropped, and kept in the record with its drift. An accepted run is flagged, never silent.

One reading trap sits in the drift rule: it divides the range of a coefficient by its final value. For a case near zero lift the final \(c_l\) is tiny, so a tiny absolute wander becomes a huge relative drift. That is an artefact of the normalisation, not a moving solution. So a drift must be read beside the coefficient it is relative to.

Level 1 — the idea (high school)

The problem. You drive on a road that is 20 miles long. Five thermometers are on fence posts at miles 2, 5, 9, 10.5 and 12. You want the temperature at all other points. Between two posts that agree, you can make a good estimate. Far from all posts, you know very little. A good model must give two things at each point: a best estimate and a statement of how sure it is.

A Gaussian process (GP) gives these two things. In this study, the "road" is the six-dimensional design box. A "thermometer" is one SU2 run or one low-fidelity run. A run is expensive, so the GP fills the space between the runs.

The bell curve. The GP describes each unknown value with a bell curve (a normal or Gaussian distribution). Two numbers define a bell curve. The mean is the centre: the best estimate. The standard deviation (sd) is the width: the uncertainty. The variance is the sd squared. The code stores variances. People usually speak in standard deviations.

Two values that move together. Think of the heights of two siblings. Each height is a bell curve. But if the older sibling is tall, the younger sibling is probably tall too. The two heights are correlated. The correlation is a number from −1 to +1.

Now you measure the older sibling. Your estimate for the younger sibling changes in two ways. The mean moves toward the measurement. The sd becomes smaller. This update is called conditioning. For bell curves, the result of conditioning is always another bell curve. That is why the GP mathematics is exact and fast.

Worked example, level 1 (by hand)

Use standard units: each height is in sd from the mean, so each sd is 1. The correlation is \(\rho = 0.8\). The older sibling is \(x_1 = 1.5\).

\[ \text{new mean} = \rho\, x_1 = 0.8 \times 1.5 = 1.2, \qquad \text{new sd} = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} = \sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 0.6 \]

Two facts are important. First, the new mean moves only 80 % of the way, because the correlation is 0.8 and not 1. Second, the new sd does not use \(x_1\). The uncertainty depends on the correlation only. It does not depend on the value that you measured. Remember this fact. It comes back in level 2.

Check, level 1
A model says "endurance 6.1 h, sd 0.2 h". What range is it about 95 % sure of?

Two sd on each side: 5.7 h to 6.5 h.

Why is the band in Figure 1 narrow near the posts and wide after mile 12?

Near a post, the unknown value is strongly correlated with a measured value, so conditioning makes the sd small. After mile 12, no measured value is near, so the sd goes back to its value before any data.

Does a GP band always contain the true value?

No. At mile 19 in Figure 1 the true value is outside the 95 % band. The band is correct only if the assumptions of the model are correct for the function.

Level 2 — the method (undergraduate)

From two values to a function. Take the temperature at every mile: 20 values. Each value is a bell curve. Neighbouring values have a high correlation. Distant values have a low correlation. This is a 20-dimensional Gaussian. Now make the step smaller and smaller. At the limit you have a value at each point of the road: a function. The formal definition is:

A Gaussian process is a set of random values, one for each input \(\mathbf{x}\), such that any finite subset has a joint Gaussian distribution. It is a probability distribution over functions. A mean function \(m(\mathbf{x})\) and a covariance function \(k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')\) define it fully.

The covariance function is also called the kernel. It gives the covariance of the outputs at any two inputs. All the assumptions about the shape of the function are in the kernel. In this repository the mean is one constant \(c\), fitted from the data.

The kernel and the lengthscale. The repository uses the Matérn 5/2 kernel. Its correlation falls as the distance \(r\) between two inputs increases. The distance is measured in lengthscales \(\ell\). At \(r = 1\) the correlation is 0.524. At \(r = 2\) it is 0.139. A long lengthscale gives a slow, smooth function. A short lengthscale gives a fast, wiggly function. The signal variance \(\sigma_f^2\) sets how far the function goes from its mean.

Smoothness. The number in "Matérn 5/2" sets the smoothness. With 1/2, the functions are rough. With 3/2, you can differentiate them once. With 5/2, you can differentiate them twice. The squared-exponential kernel gives functions that you can differentiate without limit. Physical responses are smooth, but not that smooth. Matérn 5/2 is the usual choice for engineering responses. It is less overconfident than the squared exponential between the points.

One lengthscale per input (ARD). The study has six inputs. Each input has its own lengthscale. This is automatic relevance determination (ARD). Before the fit, the code scales each input to [0, 1]. Thus a lengthscale is in "box widths", and you can compare the six values. A short lengthscale shows an input that the output is sensitive to. A long one shows an input that does not have much effect. Entry 17 gives the fitted values.

The two posterior relations

You have \(n\) runs. Make three objects from the kernel. \(\mathbf{K}\) is the \(n \times n\) matrix of the kernel between all pairs of runs. \(\mathbf{k}_*\) is the column of the kernel between the new point and each run. \(k_{**}\) is the kernel of the new point with itself, which is \(\sigma_f^2\).

\[ \mu(\mathbf{x}_*) = c + \mathbf{k}_*^{\mathsf T}\,(\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}\,(\mathbf{y} - c), \qquad \sigma^2(\mathbf{x}_*) = k_{**} - \mathbf{k}_*^{\mathsf T}\,(\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}\,\mathbf{k}_* \]

These are the sibling relations with matrices. Read the mean in three ways:

  • Weighted surprises. \(\mathbf{y} - c\) is how much each run differs from the prior mean. The inverse matrix removes the double count of runs that are close together. \(\mathbf{k}_*\) gives more weight to runs near the new point.
  • A sum of bumps. Calculate \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - c)\) one time. Then \(\mu(\mathbf{x}_*) = c + \sum_i \alpha_i\, k(\mathbf{x}_*, \mathbf{x}_i)\): one kernel bump on each run. GP.fit stores self.alpha, and GP.predict does one multiplication.
  • Kernel ridge regression. The mean is the same as a known method that has no probability in it. The GP adds the variance.

Read the variance. Start with the prior variance \(k_{**}\). Subtract the part that the runs explain. On a run, almost all of it is explained, and the variance goes to the noise level. Far from all runs, nothing is explained, and the variance goes back to \(\sigma_f^2\). The outputs \(\mathbf{y}\) are not in this relation. For fixed hyperparameters, the uncertainty depends on where you ran, not on what you measured. Thus the max-variance rule (entry 19) is a space-filling rule. The outputs have an effect only through the refit of the hyperparameters.

Worked example, level 2 (by hand)

Two runs: \(x = 0\) gives \(y = 1\), and \(x = 1\) gives \(y = 2\). Use Matérn 5/2 with \(\ell = 1\), \(\sigma_f^2 = 1\), \(c = 0\) and no noise. Predict at \(x_* = 0.5\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{K} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.524 \\ 0.524 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{K}^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.524^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -0.524 \\ -0.524 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.378 & -0.722 \\ -0.722 & 1.378 \end{pmatrix} \\ \mathbf{k}_* &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.829 \\ 0.829 \end{pmatrix} \ (\text{distance } 0.5), \qquad \mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T} = \mathbf{k}_*^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{K}^{-1} = (0.544,\ 0.544) \\ \mu(0.5) &= 0.544 \times 1 + 0.544 \times 2 = 1.631, \qquad \sigma^2(0.5) = 1 - (0.544 \times 0.829 + 0.544 \times 0.829) = 0.099, \quad \sigma = 0.31 \end{aligned} \]

The estimate is 1.631, not the average 1.5. The two weights add to 1.087, not to 1. A GP mean is not an average of the runs. Here the prior mean is 0, and the new point has a higher correlation with each run (0.829) than the two runs have with each other (0.524). Compute the same case with the code to check your hand result.

Noise and jitter

An SU2 run is not exact. A run that stops on the Cauchy rule still has some drift in its last iterations. The model writes each run as the true value plus a small random error with variance \(\sigma_n^2\). The code adds \(\sigma_n^2\) to the diagonal of \(\mathbf{K}\). Then the mean does not have to go through every run, and it does not follow the noise.

Do not mix noise and jitter. Noise is part of the model, and the fit learns it. In the code its smallest value is NOISE_FLOOR = 1e-6 (standardised units). Jitter (JITTER = 1e-8) is a numerical safety value. It prevents a failure of the Cholesky factorisation when two runs are almost at the same point. GP.predict(noise=True) adds \(\sigma_n^2\) to the variance. Use it when you compare the band with real held-out runs, because those runs contain their own noise.

How the fit finds the hyperparameters

The hyperparameters are the lengthscales, \(\sigma_f^2\), \(\sigma_n^2\) and \(c\). The fit chooses the values that make the measured outputs most probable. This is the maximum of the log marginal likelihood:

\[ \log p(\mathbf{y}) = \underbrace{-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{y}-c)^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-c)}_{\text{data fit}}\ \underbrace{-\ \tfrac{1}{2}\log\lvert\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}\rvert}_{\text{complexity penalty}}\ -\ \tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi \]

The data-fit term is high when the data looks usual for the model. The complexity term is low for a model that can fit almost any data set, for example a model with a very short lengthscale. The best value is a balance between the two. This is Occam's razor, built into one relation. You do not need held-out data to choose the lengthscale.

What the code does. GP.fit works in log space, so all values stay positive. It starts from three lengthscales (\(e^{-1}, e^{0}, e^{1}\) box widths on all inputs), runs Adam for 300 steps on each, and keeps the best. The surface can have more than one maximum. A typical pair is "short lengthscale, small noise" (the function is wiggly and the data is exact) and "long lengthscale, large noise" (the function is smooth and the data is noisy). Every relation uses one Cholesky factor \(\mathbf{L}\), with \(\mathbf{K} + \sigma_n^2\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\mathsf T}\). The log determinant is \(2\sum_i \log L_{ii}\). The cost is of order \(n^3\) time and \(n^2\) memory. With some tens of runs this cost is very small. In this study the solver is the expensive part, not the GP.

Level 3 — theory and practice (graduate)

The weight-space view. Take a linear model \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_j w_j \phi_j(\mathbf{x})\) with features \(\phi_j\) and independent weights \(w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)\). Any set of outputs is then jointly Gaussian, so this model is a GP. Its kernel is \(k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = s^2 \sum_j \phi_j(\mathbf{x})\phi_j(\mathbf{x}')\). The other direction is the surprise. A squared-exponential or Matérn kernel is equal to an infinite number of features (Mercer's theorem: \(k = \sum_j \lambda_j \psi_j \psi_j\), with \(\lambda_j \ge 0\)). The kernel lets you use all of them with an \(n \times n\) matrix only. This is the "kernel trick".

Valid kernels. A kernel must be symmetric and positive semi-definite: each matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) has no negative eigenvalue. You can make new valid kernels: a sum (two independent parts of the function), a product (an interaction), and a positive constant times a kernel.

Three connections. (1) The posterior mean is the function of smallest norm, in the reproducing-kernel Hilbert space of \(k\), that fits the data. The noise is the regularisation strength. Thus the mean is kernel ridge regression, and a smoothing spline is a special case. (2) A neural network with one hidden layer and random weights becomes a GP when the layer becomes infinitely wide (Neal, 1996). The same is true for deep networks (the NNGP), and the training of very wide networks follows the neural tangent kernel. The MLP ensemble in surrogate/mlp.py and the GP are closer than they seem. (3) Kriging, from geostatistics (Krige, Matheron), is the same method with another name.

Choosing the next run: acquisition

Active learning uses the band to choose the next run. The loop is: fit, score each candidate, run SU2 at the best candidate, record, and fit again. An acquisition function gives the score. It must balance exploitation (a good mean) and exploration (a large variance).

Expected improvement (EI), for a minimisation. Let \(y_{\text{best}}\) be the best run so far, \(g = y_{\text{best}} - \mu\) and \(z = g/\sigma\). The expected amount by which a new run is better than \(y_{\text{best}}\) (zero if it is not better) is

\[ \mathrm{EI}(\mathbf{x}) = g\,\Phi(z) + \sigma\,\varphi(z) \]

where \(\Phi\) and \(\varphi\) are the standard normal CDF and density. The first term is high for a good mean. The second term is high for a large sd. Other rules: the probability of improvement \(\Phi(z)\) (too greedy), the lower confidence bound \(\mu - \beta\sigma\), and the maximum of \(\sigma^2\) (exploration only).

Two objectives. The study has two objectives: endurance and take-off mass. There is no single best design, but a Pareto front. The score is the expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI): the mean increase of the area that the front dominates. ehvi_mc estimates it with 128 random samples from the two predicted bell curves, one GP for each objective. surrogate/feasibility.py multiplies the EHVI by the predicted probability that SU2 converges. Entries 18 to 21 give the details and the replay results.

Two levels of fidelity

The study has a cheap model (the vortex-lattice closure) and an expensive one (with the SU2 section polar). The linear autoregressive model of Kennedy and O'Hagan (2000) connects them:

\[ y_H(\mathbf{x}) = \rho\, y_L(\mathbf{x}) + \delta(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \mu_H = \rho\,\mu_L + \mu_\delta, \qquad \sigma_H^2 = \rho^2\sigma_L^2 + \sigma_\delta^2 \]

One GP learns \(y_L\) from many cheap runs. A second GP learns the discrepancy \(\delta\) from a few expensive runs. The discrepancy is often simpler than the function. TwoLevelGP finds \(\rho\) in two steps: ordinary least squares, then generalised least squares with the covariance of the discrepancy GP. The design is nested, so the cheap value at each expensive run is measured, not predicted.

Is the band correct? Calibration

A band is a claim, and you must test it on runs that the model did not see. Two measurements: the error of the mean (RMSE), and the coverage. An 80 % interval must contain about 80 % of the held-out runs. The replay calls this cov80. Less than 80 % means that the model is overconfident. More than 80 % means that it is underconfident. The standardised residual \((y - \mu)/\sigma\) must look like a standard bell curve.

The lesson for the talk: the marginal likelihood can mix noise and signal when the runs are few. Thus the study reports coverage beside every error, and does not trust the band without a test.

Interview questions
What is a Gaussian process, in one sentence?

It is a probability distribution over functions: the outputs at any finite set of inputs are jointly Gaussian, and a mean function and a kernel define it fully.

Why Matérn 5/2 and not the squared exponential?

The squared exponential assumes functions that you can differentiate without limit, and it is often overconfident between the points. Matérn 5/2 gives functions that you can differentiate twice, which is a usual and defensible choice for smooth engineering responses.

Does the uncertainty depend on the measured values?

Not for fixed hyperparameters. It depends on where the runs are. Thus max-variance sampling is space-filling, and it behaves much like a Latin hypercube. The outputs have an effect only through the refit of the hyperparameters.

Why three restarts?

The marginal likelihood can have more than one maximum, for example "wiggly function, exact data" and "smooth function, noisy data". The code starts from three lengthscales and keeps the best likelihood.

How do you know that the bands are correct?

I test them on held-out runs: an 80 % interval must contain about 80 % of them. The replay records this as cov80. With few runs a fitted GP can be overconfident, because it can explain noise as wiggles.

When does the two-level model not help?

When the difference between the cheap and the expensive model is not a scale factor plus a smooth correction: for example, when the cheap model does not have a physical effect in part of the space. Then the correction needs as many expensive runs as the function itself. I measure it on held-out designs against a single GP at the same budget.

Why not a neural network?

With some tens of expensive runs, a GP gives a band that comes from a stated model and that goes back to the prior far from the data. A deep ensemble gives a spread between networks, which can be too narrow far from the data. The replay compares both with the same interface (surrogate/mlp.py).

In Figure 10, why does EI choose 1.52 and max variance choose 0.24?

EI uses the mean and the sd: near 1.5 the mean is below the best run and the sd is not zero. Max variance uses the sd only, so it goes to the centre of the largest gap between runs.

Its marginal likelihood selects a short lengthscale and a small noise, so it fits the noise. The predicted sd of a new run is then smaller than the real scatter. With 30 runs the noise estimate is near the true value and the coverage is near the stated level.

Definition

The study has two objectives: maximise endurance and minimise take-off mass. In minimisation form they are \(f_1 = -\)endurance_h and \(f_2 = \ln\) mtow_kg. Design \(j\) dominates design \(i\) when it is no worse in both and strictly better in one. The Pareto front is the set of designs that no other design dominates. The hypervolume is the area in the \((f_1, f_2)\) plane that the front dominates, bounded by a reference point worse than every design. Inlet entry 20 explains dominance, the sweep and the staircase in full.

The exact front, and why it exists here. In the inlet study only the SU2 runs could be compared, so every front was the front of a sample. The drone's low-fidelity model costs under a second per design, so the spec (section 8) asked for the whole box on a \(9^6\) grid: 531,441 designs, each evaluated and gate-checked. The front of those clean designs is exact on the grid. It is not exact over the continuous box: a design between grid nodes can, in principle, beat it slightly, and the online replay reports such a case as a slightly negative regret instead of clipping it. This exact front is the reference that the online replay measures regret against (entry 21).

Three fronts, three meanings. (1) The exact grid front: 249 designs. (2) The front of the 192-point low pool: 15 designs; it is the reference of the pool replay (entry 20). The band. The code draws 50 joint samples of both GPs on 1,500 of the candidates, takes each sample's front, and reads the best endurance at or below each of 25 mass levels. The 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles over samples form the band. A narrow band means the two GPs agree about the trade-off.

Definition

An acquisition function scores every candidate design from the GP posterior, and the design with the largest score is evaluated next. EHVI (expected hypervolume improvement) is the mean gain in hypervolume if the candidate were evaluated, averaged over the GP's uncertainty about its two objectives. Max-variance scores a candidate by the sum of its two standardised posterior variances: it learns the whole box and ignores the front. The baselines do not use a model: random picks a candidate uniformly; Latin hypercube sampling (LHS) spreads its points so that every input's range is covered evenly. Inlet entries 21 and 22 explain these in full.

Where the candidates come from. In the pool replay the candidates are the unused points of a fixed pool, which itself is a scrambled Sobol sequence (designs/make_pool_drone.py). In the online replay every step draws a fresh set of 4,096 scrambled Sobol points over the whole box (candidate_set), and nothing removes a candidate before the pick. A Sobol sequence is a low-discrepancy sequence: it fills the box more evenly than independent random draws.

Two LHS variants. LHS is not sequential. In the pool replay, lhs_select draws a fresh LHS of \(n\) points (optimisation random-cd) and matches each to a distinct nearest pool point by a minimum-cost assignment. In the online replay, LHS draws a fresh design of \(n\) points over the box at every report budget and evaluates them all.

The feasibility-weighted variants. al_ehvi_feas and al_maxvar_feas multiply the score by \(p(\text{feasible} \mid \mathbf{x})\) from a logistic regression (entry 21). The classifier weights; it never picks and never removes a candidate.

Who chooses. The acquisition function chooses, from the GP posterior and a seeded random generator. The language model never chooses a sampling point; this is a repository rule (CLAUDE.md), and the code path shows it. The MCP server's 11 tools validate, build, stage and ingest cases; its docstring says it "never runs SU2 and never chooses sampling points", and queue_batch takes a design CSV written by a script. Every strategy is deterministic given the seed, so every replay row reproduces.

Definition

Definition

The online replay removes the pool. At every step each strategy picks one design from 4,096 fresh Sobol candidates over the whole box, and the pick is evaluated with the real low-fidelity model. Nothing filters the candidates. A pick that fails a gate, or whose mass closure fails, uses up one evaluation, is counted in n_gated, and enters no model and no hypervolume. Regret is measured against the exact front of the \(9^6\) grid. Ten seeds, 8 initial points (the first 8 gate-passing candidates of step 0, the same for every strategy of a seed), 40 evaluations in total.

The first run: active learning loses. Four strategies (random, LHS, EHVI, max-variance). Plain EHVI ends at regret 0.317 after 40 evaluations, worse than random (0.294) and LHS (0.271). The mechanism is in the record: EHVI spends 29.7 of its 32 picks on designs that fail a gate. The front lies against the gated edge of the box (long span, high aspect ratio, low taper; entry 18), and the two GPs know nothing about the gates. Worse, a gated pick enters no model, so the GP posterior does not change and the next pick goes to the same region again. Max-variance does the same for a different reason: the variance is largest where nothing has been observed, which is the gated region.

The second run: the feasibility model. The study's response was a fifth and sixth strategy, run beside the first four and not instead of them, on the same seeds, candidate sets and initial designs. A logistic regression on the six scaled inputs gives \(p(\text{feasible} \mid \mathbf{x})\). It is fitted before each pick on every point evaluated so far for that strategy and seed: clean points have label 1, gated or failed points label 0, and the draws rejected while building the initial design also count as 0. Until both labels exist, the factor is 1. The acquisition score is multiplied by \(p\).

The honest conclusion. Both runs are published and neither replaces the other. Active learning beats space-filling sampling on this problem only when the acquisition knows where the design is buildable, and loses to it otherwise. A caveat travels with it: here a gated pick costs one low-model evaluation, so the replay is a deliberately harsh test; in the SU2 pipeline the gates run before submission, so a gated design costs seconds, not a CFD run.

It only multiplies the acquisition score by the probability. It never picks a point and never removes a candidate; the argmax of the weighted EHVI picks. With it, gated picks fall from 29.7 to 7.5 and the regret at 40 falls from 0.317 to 0.092, below random and LHS in all 10 seeds.

The gates are cheap and closed form. Why not just filter the candidates?

In the real pipeline I do: the gates run before any SU2 submission, and the Pareto and inverse analyses only use gate-passing candidates. The online replay leaves the candidates unfiltered on purpose, to test whether the acquisition itself learns where designs are buildable, and it counts a gated pick as a lost evaluation. That is a harsh test, and I state it as such. Some gates need the full mass closure, so a learned boundary is still useful when the gates are not all closed form.

Definition

The two-level (multi-fidelity) model is the linear autoregressive form of Kennedy and O'Hagan, often called AR1 or co-kriging. It says the expensive answer is a scaled copy of the cheap answer plus a smooth correction: \(y_H = \rho\, y_L + \delta\). One GP is fitted to every low-fidelity design. A second GP is fitted to the discrepancy \(\delta\) at the SU2 designs. The design is nested: every SU2 design is also a low-pool design, so \(y_L\) is observed at those points, not predicted. Here the low level is the vortex-lattice closure with closed-form section data; the high level is the same closure with the SU2 section polar (decision 8: the medium-mesh SA-BCM polar, accepted with the V6e lift-slope failure recorded beside it).

The question it answers. Spec question 3: does the two-level model reach a given held-out error with fewer SU2 designs than a GP on SU2 designs alone? For each budget \(n\) and seed, \(n\) SU2 designs are drawn at random and the rest are held out. The single GP and the two-level model are fitted on the same \(n\) points. The reports are held-out RMSE, the 90 % interval coverage of a new run, and \(\rho\). The error of the raw low-fidelity value, used as a prediction with no model, is reported once.

What exists, and what it can say. The budget sweep ran on all 32 SU2 designs (mf.json: usable_high 32, budgets 8, 12, 16 and 24). A budget must leave at least 4 held-out designs, so 32 is dropped. At budget 24 each seed holds out 8 designs, so a coverage fraction there is resolved only in steps of \(1/8 = 0.125\). The earlier readings at 13, 15 and 30 designs are kept in the task file as dated blocks; the 32-design reading replaces them as the study's answer.

Definition

The inverse query asks for the design with the longest predicted endurance whose take-off mass is at most 4.0 kg with probability at least 0.9. The 4.0 kg cap is the EASA open-category class C2 limit. The cap and the probability were fixed in the spec (section 10.4) before any run. The probability comes from the \(\ln m\) GP: \(\ln m\) is Gaussian, so \(P(m \le m_{\mathrm{cap}})\) is one normal CDF. The search runs on a \(21^6\) grid inside the gates. The answer is then confirmed by one SU2 evaluation (five SU2 cases); the C2 check passes if both simulated objectives fall inside their predicted 90 % intervals.

Walking 85.8 million points. The grid is never held in memory. It is walked in chunks of 50,000. Each chunk first goes through the closed-form gates (tip chord, spar depth). The endurance mean is cheap, so it is computed for every surviving point; the mass GP and the probability are computed only for points whose mean beats the 200th best so far, since no other point can enter the list. The full closure gates (about 0.5 s each) run on the 200 best in order, and the first to pass is the answer. A test checks that this pruning gives the same answer as predicting every point.

The low-fidelity answer, and what it means. On the GPs of the 192-point low pool the answer is a box corner with all six inputs at a bound. The cap never binds there. This is a diagnostic, for two stated reasons. First, the low model's heaviest front design is 2.44 kg, so the low-fidelity answer is simply the longest-endurance buildable corner and question 4 is only tested at the high level. Second, the GP is extrapolating: the Sobol pool has no point on the box bounds. The SU2-level answer and its confirmation are worked below the low-fidelity example.

Definition

Definition

A solver that exits with code 0 has only said that it stopped without a crash. It has not said that the answer is converged, that the files are the ones we expect, or that the number agrees with the physics. The first SU2 airfoil case of the drone study shows this. Definition

Two workers that serve one queue without a rule will both start the same job. The reason is timing: the worker writes 1-running only after it has rebuilt the mesh, and Drive sync adds tens of seconds, so the second worker still sees the job as new. This is a claim race. The shard rule removes the race instead of trying to win it. Each worker gets a fixed share of the case ids, decided by the id alone. With --shard K/N a worker takes only the jobs whose id hash, read as an integer, is K modulo N. The shares do not overlap and together they cover every id, so no two workers ever look at the same job, and no lock is needed.

Interview questions
Why not use a lock file to claim a job?

Drive is not a file system with atomic locks. A file one worker writes can take tens of seconds to appear for the other worker, so two workers can both see "no lock" and both take the job. A shard needs no communication at all: each worker can decide from the case id alone whether a job is its own. The rule cannot race because there is nothing to race for. The cost is that a worker cannot take over another shard's jobs if that worker stops.

What happens if one sharded worker dies?

Its jobs wait. The other worker never sees them, because the filter is applied before the status is read. When I restart the dead shard, its stale 1-running job is found by the heartbeat rule and run again. If I wanted the surviving worker to finish everything, I would restart it with --shard 0/1, which is every job. That is safe only after the other worker is really stopped.

Interview questions
Why keep the wrong statement in the record at all?

Because the correction is only credible if you can see what changed and why. If I rewrote the sentence in place, a reader could not tell a correction on evidence from a number moved to look better. Each entry says what was first written, what the record now says, and what found the error, usually the deck builder reading the run files directly. None of them moved a tolerance after a verdict, and the V6e lift slope is still a fail.

Your lift slope fails by half a point. Why not widen the tolerance to 6 %?

Because the 5 % was fixed before any case ran, and moving it after the verdict would make the check worthless. The miss is reported as a fail. Éder ruled to accept the medium-mesh polar as the high level with the bias recorded, so every SU2 polar carries the flag with the recorded deviation and verdict. The two-level model then has a known bias to absorb, and the report says so beside every high-level number.
